\section{Introduction}\label{sec1}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

Local governments control urban pests with small teams, limited budgets and a restricted set of resources such as traps, chemicals, staff and vehicles, so they must decide where to act first. The decision matters for public health. Cockroaches carry pathogens and parasites \citep{kalantari2023first,abdullah2024cockroaches} and are associated with allergic sensitisation and asthma \citep{liccardi2023narrative}, and the cost of pest control is substantial: 61 of the 142 National Health Service trusts in the United Kingdom spent \pounds3.7 million on it between 2021 and 2023 \citep{libdems}. A municipality that receives citizen reports of sightings, such as the vector control unit of Madrid, can use them to decide where to place traps or to inspect first. This paper asks how to build such a priority list from the reports and from what is known about each area of the city.

The usual tools for this question are hot-spot statistics. The Getis--Ord statistic $G_i^*$ \citep{getis1992analysis,ord1995local} compares the sum of a variable around each area with its expected value, and the local Moran's $I$ \citep{anselin1995local} detects areas whose values resemble those of their neighbours. Scan statistics \citep{kulldorff1997spatial} search for the circular window with the largest excess of cases, and disease-mapping models \citep{besag1991bayesian} smooth relative risks with a spatially structured random effect. For urban pests in Madrid, \citet{tamayo2014risk} and \citet{ayyad2018non,ayyad2019trivariate} modelled the intensity of rat sightings as a function of environmental risk factors and produced risk maps from the fitted values.

Three features of report data are not fully addressed by these tools. First, the number of reports in an area depends on how many people live there and can see a pest, so a map of counts mostly shows where people live; a useful hot spot is an area with more reports than its characteristics predict. Second, local statistics are computed for every area of the map, so some areas are labelled hot by chance even when there is no spatial structure; the error control should apply to the map as a whole. Third, a priority list should be judged by what it achieves, with the true hot areas known in simulation and with future reports in the application, and it should be compared with the simpler alternatives that a practitioner would otherwise use.

We propose a procedure that addresses these three points. A hot spot is defined as an area where the variation not explained by the covariates is positive and shared with its neighbours (Section~\ref{sec:setting}). The procedure first tests whether the residuals of the regression on the covariates have spatial structure, with Moran's $I$ and its exact moments \citep{tg07}, and stops if they do not. It then estimates the spatial component with eigenvector spatial filtering (ESF) \citep{grif2000,grif03}, using the smooth eigenvectors of the contiguity matrix and a ridge penalty chosen by cross-validation, labels each area hot, cold or neutral with a permutation band, and orders the areas into a priority list. Each of these choices is motivated with simulations in Section~\ref{sec:sim-choices}.

The contributions of the paper are the following. We give a definition of covariate-adjusted hot and cold spots and a procedure to estimate them with a test before mapping. We compare the procedure with $G_i^*$, local Moran's $I$ and rankings without spatial smoothing in a simulation study with nine scenarios that vary the size, shape and number of the clusters and include a cold area, a missing covariate and data without clusters. We apply the procedure to cockroach sightings in Madrid in 2010--2013, with nested models that separate exposure (how many people can report), environmental risk factors and reporting habits (rat reports in the same areas), and we evaluate the priority lists on the following summer's reports of cockroachs. The method is implemented in an R package called \texttt{hotcoldESF} available in the project repository.

The rest of the paper is organised as follows. Section~\ref{sec:met} describes the procedure and the rationale for each step. Section~\ref{sec:sim} presents the simulation study and Section~\ref{sec:app} the application to Madrid. Section~\ref{sec:conc} discusses when to use the procedure, its limitations and possible extensions.
