\begin{abstract}
Municipalities that control urban pests need to decide where to act first, and citizen reports of sightings are often the only data available. Reports, however, mostly follow where people live. We define a hot spot as an area with more reports than its local characteristics predict, shared with its neighbours, and propose a procedure to detect and map suchmap such hot and cold spots and to further rank areas for intervention. The procedure tests the residuals of a regression on the covariates for spatial structure for spatial structre, and estimates the spatial; estimates the spatial component by eigenvector spatial filtering,using the smooth eigenvectors of the contiguity matrix and a ridge penalty chosen by cross-validation. The our procedure labels each area hot, cold or neutral with a permutation band, and orders the hot areas into a priority list. % In a simulation study with nine scenarios, the procedure was as precise as the Getis--Ord $G_i^*$ statistic and the local Moran's $I$ with strong clusters, found clusters of different sizes more often, and was less precise with weak compact clusters; the test before mapping reduced the share of the map falsely labelled hot from about 5\% to at most 2\% for all methods. 
Applied to cockroach sightings in Madrid in 2010--2013, with nested models for exposure, environmental risk factors and rat reports, the procedure found hot zones that persisted across summers and differed from those of rats once exposure was accounted for. Evaluated on the following summer, ranking by counts captured the most reports, and ranking the residuals of single cells captured more of the excess over the covariates than the smoothed map; we suggest using the hot zones to decide where to act and the residuals to order cells within them.
\end{abstract}
